A ground-powered head start for rockets, and what it is really worth

launch-assist-sim is a research simulator for ground-powered launch assist. A rocket gets its first tens of metres per second from electrically powered ground hardware: so far, a vertical maglev silo that pushes a Falcon 9-class rocket out at about 77 m/s before or while its engines light. The simulator then flies the same rocket to orbit and measures what the push is worth in payload, propellant, losses, loads, energy and peak power. Every result is a difference against the same rocket launched from a pad, and the results that undercut the idea are reported as plainly as the ones that support it.

  • concept A: vertical maglev silo
  • planar 2-D, rotating Earth
  • Falcon 9-class gate vehicle
  • 200 km circular, 28.5°

The question

The question

With the payload and the orbit held fixed, how much of a rocket's propellant can a ground-powered push replace?

Kerbal Space Program, OpenRocket and RocketPy all start a rocket from a pad or a short launch rail, so none of them can answer it. Here the launch-assist phase is a swappable model, and the rocket after release is the same rocket, flying its own sweep-optimized ascent.

The hypothesis under test: any ground-supplied velocity, however small, cuts the propellant the rocket needs, or raises its payload. The simulator's real job is to measure the penalties that can eat that gain.

Answer so far, on the planar model

Preliminary finding · phase SP1 Pre-registered · run 2026-10-03

41.26 t

of stage-1 propellant left out: 10.04% of the stage-1 load (410.9 t) and 7.96% of the total (518.4 t), with the pad's payload (26,054.4 kg) still delivered to the same 200 km circular orbit at 28.5°.

The case: the 3 g / 100 m cold-start silo (released at 76.7 m/s, engines lit 0.5 s later with a 2 s ramp) on the gate vehicle model, sweep-optimized and unthrottled. The offloaded vehicle's own payload search returns the pad's payload to within 0.01 kg, and the pad itself can leave out nothing. Under the ±10% sensitivity arms (stage-1 dry mass, Isp, CD, drive efficiency) it stays between 38.67 and 44.46 t. It is 2.76× the ideal-screening estimate (14.98 t), and the loss breakdown explains the beat.

Read it with these, beside the number

  • A vehicle model, not a Falcon 9. The gate vehicle calibrates +14.3% high. On the README-loads fork, which calibrates inside the band (+8.3%), the same case leaves out 36.01 t, 9.10% of its stage-1 load: read it as 9–10% of stage 1 across the two models.
  • Before structural mass. Nothing is charged for the 4 g full-stack push. An assumed +8.1 t of stage-1 dry mass leaves 1.98 t, and about 8.5 t (extrapolated) cancels it.
  • Much of it is the lighter stack, not the push. Read as the pre-registration worded it, most of the offload is the lighter stack's thrust-to-weight: the pad flown with the same offload falls only 1.4 t short. A delta-v reading chosen after the run gives the lighter stack 28–29% (below).
  • Loads. The offloaded run's unthrottled max-Q is 3.4% above the pad's (38.4 against 37.2 kPa): the full-load silo's 16% reduction is spent. q-alpha at the kick is 3.0× the pad's, and the stack feels 4.0 g on the track.
  • The model. Sweep-optimized guidance, no throttle, a free kick (no angle-of-attack aerodynamics), a prescribed 3 g drive with a massless carriage and a vented shaft, and partly filled tanks with every dry mass unchanged.

Head start or lighter stack?

The pad flown with the same 41.26 t offload and no push carries 24,652.4 kg, 1,402.0 kg short of the pad's payload, so the push is needed to carry the offload.

As pre-registered That shortfall is 3.4% of the offload, which is small, so the pre-registered branch fires: most of the offload is the lighter stack's thrust-to-weight, not the head start. The note reports this verdict, and argues that it compares payload kilograms with propellant kilograms.

Read after the run In ideal delta-v, a reading chosen after the run, the lighter stack's own thrust-to-weight gain is 28–29% of the 228.2 m/s saved; the release speed and the head start's trajectory effects are 64–66%, and the pad's hold-down convention 6–7%.

The bridge: the README masses

The robustness check against the calibration miss: a vehicle with other stage masses that calibrates inside the ±10% band (+8.3%). The same case leaves out 36.01 t, 9.10% of its 395.7 t stage-1 load (2.53× its screening estimate).

That is 9.4% lower, in relative terms, than on the gate vehicle: inside the pre-registered agreement band of about 10%, near its edge. Its paired pad (the same offload, no push) falls 1,309.5 kg short of that fork's payload, 3.6% of the 36.01 t offload in kilograms (derived). The findings note applies the pre-registered small-margin reading to the headline only, not to the bridge. In ideal delta-v, a reading chosen after the run, the lighter stack is 26–27% of the bridge's 208.67 m/s (28–29% on the gate vehicle).

Whether the gate vehicle's calibration miss inflates its figure is not established.

  • Stage 1 is the only headline. The stage-2 case (31.90 t gross) failed its independent verification, so it is a flagged lower bound; as pre-registered, stage-2 and both-stage numbers are labelled a property of the vehicle model.
  • The energy ratio is not an efficiency claim. The 12.4 t of RP-1 removed carry about 128× the push's 1.16 MWh of metered electricity in combustion heat (5.65× with the assumed +8.1 t). The ratio of unlike quantities leaves out LOX production, fuel supply, grid losses and the facility.
  • Depth and ignition. At a fixed exit speed the offload is the same by construction in this model (50 to 300 m of stroke within 0.07 kg); where the thrust ramp starts moves it from 26.7 to 47.0 t, under the prescribed drive.

Source: RQ1-fuel-offload-2d (the findings note, caveats first) and the README's Fuel replaced at fixed payload (SP1). Pre-registered in docs/phases/inputs; run from commit b3150c1.

How it works

One run is a sequence of phases joined by events: an optional hold, the push along the track, release, ignition, the ascent, staging and orbit insertion. Every silo run is flown again as a pad launch of the same rocket.

  1. Step 1

    The push

    The rocket stands in an underground shaft lined with electromagnetic rings that act as a vertical linear motor. In the reference case the drive pushes the full stack 100 m at 3 g net and releases it at 76.7 m/s after 2.6 s. Engines light in the shaft (a hot start) or just after release (a cold start).

    Today the drive imposes a prescribed acceleration (constant_accel) with an unbounded force and a massless carriage; a force- and power-limited linear motor is planned for phase SP7, after SP2.

  2. Step 2

    The same rocket's ascent

    At release the state is mapped into a planar ascent over a rotating, spherical Earth with the standard atmosphere, drag that varies with Mach and nozzle back-pressure: a vertical rise and kick, a gravity turn on stage 1, staging, then linear-tangent steering on stage 2 to a 200 km circular orbit at 28.5°. A bisection finds the payload capacity, the payload that leaves zero propellant at insertion.

  3. Step 3

    Compared with a pad launch

    The baseline is the same vehicle launched from a pad, with the same guidance law, sweep grid and search budget. Every number is a difference against it. The losses are booked so that speed gained equals vacuum Δv minus gravity, drag, steering and back-pressure losses; a validation test requires that budget to close within 0.01 m/s over a full ascent, and it passes.

Results so far

Everything here is preliminary. It covers concept A only (the vertical silo, with a drive that imposes a prescribed acceleration) on the planar 2-D model, with guidance that is sweep-optimized, not optimal-control. Each number is copied from a findings note, and its main caveat sits beside it. The full set is in the README's Results so far.

Propellant replaced at fixed payload

41.26 t of stage-1 propellant

10.04% of the stage-1 load and 7.96% of the total, with the pad's 26,054.4 kg still delivered to the 200 km orbit: the 3 g / 100 m cold-start silo. Under the ±10% sensitivity arms it stays between 38.67 and 44.46 t; on the README masses (calibrated inside the band) it is 36.01 t, 9.10%.

Caveat: before structural mass (about 8.5 t of assumed stage-1 dry mass, extrapolated, cancels it); on the gate vehicle model (+14.3%); sweep-optimized and unthrottled, with max-Q 3.4% above the pad's. Payload gain, cold-start silo

+1,498.8 kg

+5.75% over the pad's 26,054.4 kg to a 200 km circular orbit: 3 g net over 100 m, released at 76.7 m/s, engines lit 0.5 s later with a 2 s ramp. Under every ±10% sensitivity case the gain stays between +1,345 and +1,667 kg.

Caveat: no structural mass is charged for the 4.0 g full-stack push, and about 8.1 t of stage-1 strengthening would cancel the whole gain. The gate vehicle calibrates +14.3% high; on a vehicle calibrated to 22.8 t the gain would be expected near 1.3 t (derived by proportion, not run).

Source: RQ3-silo-screening-2d

Against the hand estimate

About 2.0×

the ideal rocket-equation estimate (765.4 kg at the pad's payload). A vertical release is not constant-loss: in the matched basis the gain splits into 46% release speed at face value, 34% lower gravity and steering loss, 9% the pad's hold-down burn, 8% back-pressure and 2% drag. The breakdown explains the beat, so it is not treated as a bug.

Caveat: the trajectory share is unverified beyond the gravity-turn family; optimal control could move it in either direction. The pad's 2.7 t clamped ramp is a baseline convention: against a pad lit at release the gain is +1,415.8 kg.

Source: RQ3-silo-screening-2d

Lighting the engines after release

−327.1 kg

(34.74 m/s) given back by the cold start against an instant start: 18% of the instant start's gain and 2.37× the 1-D formula. A 3 s lag after the same 0.5 s delay gives back 781 kg. Lighting on the carriage, with the ramp ending at release, is the best physical variant: +235.0 kg over the cold start, paid for with 2.7 t burned on the track.

Caveat: under a prescribed acceleration a hot start cannot add exit speed, so the hot-start question waits for a force-limited drive. The hot ramp has no sensitivity run.

Source: RQ2-ignition-timing-2d

Aerodynamic loads

−16.0% max-Q

Unthrottled max-Q falls from 37.19 to 31.24 kPa, and the drag loss falls 3.5 m/s. But q-alpha at the kick rises to 1.7–3.4× the pad's (130–251 Pa rad at 77 m/s), and the model charges no penalty for it.

Caveat: nothing is throttled and there is no angle-of-attack aerodynamics, so max-Q and q-alpha are upper bounds; max-Q sits at the standard atmosphere's tropopause kink (11.02 km). The −16.0% is at full load: once the push is cashed in as propellant (the 41.26 t offload), the silo run reaches 38.4 kPa, 3.4% above the pad's 37.2 kPa.

Source: RQ6-aero-2d-preliminary; at the offload: RQ1-fuel-offload-2d

Ground power

1.725 GW peak

drive power at release, after a 2.6 s push. Each launch takes 2.249 GJ (1,249.5 kWh of electricity at an assumed 50%), and the facility is 160 m long: a 100 m stroke plus 60 m of braking at 5 g. The facility is sized by power, not energy.

Caveat: the drive force is unbounded, the carriage is massless and the vented shaft has no air drag (which biases energy, peak power and interface force low by about 0.04%, 0.08% and 0.08%).

Source: RQ3-silo-screening-2d

Loads on the full stack

4.0 g felt

by the fully fuelled stack during the push: 3.996 g and 22.49 MN at the interface on the 573.9 t 2-D stack. Rockets normally see such g only near burnout, with nearly empty tanks (the 1-D stage 1 feels 5.71 g at burnout, at 146.87 t).

Caveat: there is no structural model yet. The strengthening this load needs is the open item that decides whether the gain survives (phase SP7, after SP2).

Source: RQ3-silo-screening-2d, RQ3-silo-screening-1d

Calibration (labelled calibration, not validation)

+14.3% high

The gate vehicle reaches 26,054.4 kg to 200 km circular at 28.5° against the published 22,800 kg: 974.4 kg above the ±10% band, a miss. Nothing was tuned toward the published number. The user accepted the miss as documented on 2026-09-30.

Caveat: the cause is not established, since the omissions point both ways (throttling, reserves and residuals would lower the payload; better guidance could raise it). Every 2-D number inherits the miss, so read results as differences between runs.

Caveats that apply to every 2-D number

  • Guidance is sweep-optimized, not optimal-control.
  • Nothing is throttled, so max-Q and q-alpha are upper bounds.
  • The kick is free: there is no angle-of-attack aerodynamics.
  • The gate vehicle's +14.3% calibration miss carries into every number.
  • No structural mass is charged for the 4 g full-stack load.
  • The drive is prescribed-acceleration (constant_accel), with an unbounded drive force. A hot start therefore cannot add exit speed, and carriage mass and drive efficiency move only energy and power.
  • The carriage has zero mass except in the 22 t sled variant.
  • There is no air drag in the vented shaft.
  • The model is a planar point mass with a generic CD(Mach) table.

What goes against the hypothesis

Stated as plainly as the results that support it. Every item is from the README's Results so far and the findings notes it cites.

Propellant at fixed payload (SP1)

  • Structure can cancel the offload. Each tonne of assumed stage-1 strengthening takes 4.5–5.1 t off the 41.26 t. At +8.1 t only 1.98 t (0.48% of stage 1) remains, and about 8.5 t (extrapolated) takes all of it. How much structure a 4 g full-stack push needs is unknown in this model.
  • Part of the headline is not the push. 14.4 m/s of the 228.2 m/s of ideal delta-v saved (6.3%) is the pad's clamped hold-down burn, a baseline convention. By a difference between runs, another 28–29% is the lighter stack's own thrust-to-weight gain, which the push makes usable but does not produce; read as pre-registered, most of the offload is the lighter stack.
  • The max-Q benefit disappears. The full-load silo's 16% lower max-Q becomes 3.35% above the pad's once the push is cashed in as propellant, and up to 12.5% above at a 300 m stroke; q-alpha at the kick is 3.0× the pad's. Nothing throttles or limits either.
  • Diminishing returns with depth. The offload per m/s of exit speed falls from 622 to 429 kg as the stroke grows from 25 to 300 m at 3 g. At a fixed exit speed a gentler push costs up to 71% more electricity and a longer facility for the same offload.
  • A late start is expensive. Each second of ignition delay after release costs 4.8–5.3 t of offload.
  • The figure depends on the vehicle masses. On the README-loads fork, which calibrates inside the band, the offload is 9.10% of stage 1 rather than 10.04%.
  • Stage 2 is not a headline. The stage-2 case failed its independent verification (a flagged lower bound), and why its figure is so large is unresolved.

Payload at full load (Phase 2)

  • Structural mass could cancel the gain. No structural mass is charged for the 4.0 g full-stack load (22.5 MN at the interface). At about 184 kg of payload per tonne of stage-1 dry mass, about 8.1 t of silo-only strengthening would cancel the whole gain (a linear extrapolation, not a sized structure).
  • Part of the headline is not the push. The pad's 2.7 t clamped ramp is a baseline convention, and it accounts for 130.7 kg gross (83.1 kg net). Against a pad lit at release, the gain is +1,415.8 kg.
  • The calibration miss inflates it. On a vehicle calibrated to 22.8 t the gain would be expected near 1.3 t. That is derived by proportion, not run.
  • The startup after release is expensive. The cold start gives back 327.1 kg (34.74 m/s) against an instant start, 2.37× the 1-D formula. A 3 s lag after the same 0.5 s delay gives back 781 kg, 43% of the instant start's gain.
  • A full hot start does not pay. silo_hot_full is 10.2 kg behind the cold start, and −18.5 to +0.1 kg under the ±10% cases: a tie, not a gain.
  • The trajectory gain is unverified beyond the gravity-turn family. It appears only after main-engine cutoff, and it is the part a better pad ascent (optimal control, README Phase 5) could change, in either direction.
  • Small pushes gain little from the push itself. At the slow corner of the sweep (0.5 g, 50 m, 22 m/s) the trajectory term is negative (−11.1 m/s), and about 127 kg gross (about 83 kg net, 35%) of that point's +240 kg is the pad's hold-down burn, not the push.
  • One aerodynamic load gets worse. q-alpha at the kick is above the pad's in every silo variant: 130–251 Pa rad at 77 m/s, up to 3,409 Pa rad at 172 m/s. The model charges no penalty for it.
  • A failed ignition has no abort. The full stack returns to the shaft mouth at 76.6 m/s after 15.7 s, 0.4 m from the axis.
  • The facility is sized by power. A 1.725 GW peak at release after a 2.6 s push, 2.249 GJ per launch, and a 160 m facility (100 m stroke and 60 m of braking at 5 g).

Not a new idea, and not the first to try

Ground-assisted launch has a long history, and the closest precedents shape this work. What this project adds is a measurement tool: a simulator in which the assist is a swappable model and every result is a difference against the same rocket launched from a pad. The full table is the README's Prior art.

  • NASA Magnetic Launch Assist / MagLifter1990s–2000s. A horizontal maglev track about 1.5 miles long, 600 mph in 9.5 s; technology demos only. Its "over 20%" fuel-saving figure is treated as a claim to explain, never a number to tune toward.
  • EMALS, U.S. NavyA 91 m linear motor launches a 45 t aircraft to 240 km/h from four 121 MJ flywheel alternators. Operational: it proves the ~100 m, ~70 m/s regime at 45 t.
  • Long March 11, ChinaA 58 t solid-fuel launcher cold-launched from a tube, igniting after ejection. Operational: "eject, then ignite" works at 58 t with solid motors.
  • Radian AerospaceA rocket-powered rail sled releases a winged spaceplane at Mach 0.7 with its engines already lit. In development; wings make a shallow release workable.
  • Holloman maglev sled, U.S. Air ForceA rocket-propelled superconducting maglev sled reached 633 mph on a 2,100 ft track (2016).
  • DARPA/NASA Horizontal Launch StudyReviewed 130+ horizontal-launch studies spanning 60 years (2011). Only Pegasus became operational: many concepts close on paper, and cost decides.
